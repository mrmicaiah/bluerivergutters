The Solution

What Are Underground Gutter Drains?

Underground gutter drains are buried PVC pipes that connect to your downspouts and route roof water away from your foundation. Instead of dumping water 4-6 feet from your house (where it often works its way back toward the foundation in clay soil), underground drains carry it 15-30 feet away — or to a proper discharge point like the street, a drainage swale, or a dry well.

The system is simple: an adapter connects to your existing downspout, transitions underground into a 4" PVC pipe buried 8-12 inches deep, and runs with a slight slope to a discharge point. At the end, a pop-up emitter opens when water flows through and closes when dry — keeping debris and pests out.

Once installed, the only visible evidence is a small emitter head in your lawn. No extensions to trip over, nothing to move for mowing, no ugly plastic running across your yard. Just invisible, permanent drainage.

Underground Drains vs. French Drains

These are different solutions for different problems. Underground gutter drains handle roof water — they connect to downspouts. French drains handle groundwater that seeps through soil. Many Madison homes benefit from both systems working together: underground drains manage the massive volume of roof water, while French drains address subsurface water that saturates the yard.

Do You Need This?

Signs You Need Underground Drainage

Standing Water Near Foundation

If water pools within a few feet of your house after rain and takes days to dissipate, your current drainage isn't cutting it.

Erosion Channels

Ruts, channels, or washed-out areas in your landscaping where water flows. Your yard is telling you where the water goes — and it's not where you want it.

Basement or Crawlspace Moisture

Damp walls, musty smells, or actual water intrusion below grade. Water is getting in somehow, and poor surface drainage is often the culprit.

Foundation Cracks

Horizontal or stair-step cracks in foundation walls can indicate hydrostatic pressure from water-saturated soil pressing against the foundation.

Soggy Yard That Won't Dry

If parts of your yard stay muddy for days after rain while neighbors' yards dry out, you have a drainage problem — often related to downspout discharge.

Extensions Always In The Way

Tired of moving downspout extensions every time you mow? That's reason enough to go underground — convenience plus better water management.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

1

Site Assessment

We evaluate your property's drainage patterns, identify the best discharge locations, determine pipe routing, and check for underground utilities (we call 811). You'll know exactly what we're planning before we dig.

2

Excavation

We dig narrow trenches from each downspout to the discharge point. Trenches are typically 8-12 inches deep with proper slope (at least 1/8" per foot) to ensure water flows by gravity. Sod is carefully set aside for replacement.

3

Pipe Installation

We install 4" Schedule 40 PVC pipe — solid, not perforated — with glued connections at every joint. Clean-out access points are included so you can flush the system if ever needed.

4

Connection

Each downspout gets an adapter that transitions to the underground pipe. The connection is secure and watertight — no leaks dumping water right next to your foundation.

5

Discharge Setup

We install pop-up emitters (most common), daylight drains, or other discharge solutions based on your property. The goal is getting water as far from the foundation as possible.

6

Backfill & Restoration

Soil is compacted in layers to prevent settling, sod is replaced, and we clean up completely. Within a few weeks, you won't be able to tell we were there.

Customized Solutions

Underground Drain Options

Discharge Methods

  • Pop-up emitter: Opens when water flows, closes when dry. Most common choice.
  • Daylight drain: Open pipe end at property edge or slope. Simple and effective.
  • Dry well: Underground chamber that disperses water into surrounding soil.
  • Storm drain connection: Direct connection to municipal system where permitted.

Pipe Options

  • 4" PVC (standard): Handles most residential downspouts with ease.
  • 6" PVC (high-volume): For large roofs or combining multiple downspouts.
  • Corrugated: Budget option, but less durable and more prone to clogging. We don't recommend it.

Add-Ons

  • Catch basins: Collect surface water from low spots in addition to roof water.
  • Channel drains: Linear drains across driveways or patios.
  • French drain integration: Combine with perimeter drains for complete drainage solution.
