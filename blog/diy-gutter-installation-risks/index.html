Why Hanging Gutters Yourself is a Bad Idea

2021-01-06 4 min read

We've replaced a lot of DIY gutter installations over the years. Here's what goes wrong and why professional installation usually makes more sense.

Hanging Gutters at the Wrong Pitch

The most common DIY mistake is improper slope. Gutters need to pitch toward the downspout — about 1/4 inch per 10 feet of run. Too little slope and water sits instead of draining. Too much slope and the gutters look crooked and can overflow at the low end during heavy rain.

The tricky part: if you're installing sectional gutters (the kind you buy at hardware stores), each section might end up angled slightly differently. Water flowing one direction hits a section angled the opposite way. The water backs up, can't find the outlet, and overflows over the sides.

We see this constantly on DIY jobs. The gutters look fine at first glance, but watch during a moderate rain and you'll see water cascading over the edges while the downspout stays bone dry.

Hanger Problems

Two mistakes here: spacing and type.

Spacing: Hangers should be installed every 24 inches for North Alabama's rainfall. Code minimum is 32 inches, and that's what most DIYers use. The difference? At 32-inch spacing, gutters sag when full of water or debris. They develop waves and low spots where water pools. Over time, those low spots become permanent, and the gutters never drain properly.

Type: Hidden hangers aren't all equal. Some are light-duty, some heavy-duty. They hold different weights. Mixing them creates inconsistent strength along the run. A full 6-inch gutter can hold almost 2 gallons per linear foot — that's about 16 pounds per foot when full. If one hanger is weaker, that section sags.

The math: A 40-foot run of 6-inch gutter, fully loaded with water and debris, weighs 640+ pounds. That weight is hanging from your fascia. Hangers need to be the right type, right spacing, and properly secured into solid wood.

Downspout Placement

Water doesn't flow around corners easily. It builds up at inside and outside corners. A professional positions downspout outlets to account for this — usually within a few feet of corners to prevent backup.

DIYers often put downspouts where they're convenient rather than where they're needed. The result: corners that overflow while the downspout a few feet away handles less water than it should.

Rule of thumb: One downspout per 35-40 linear feet of gutter. Positioning matters as much as quantity.

Material Quality Differences

Materials from hardware stores aren't the same as what professionals use. The coil aluminum at big box stores is typically .027 gauge — thin enough that it dents if you look at it wrong. Professional-grade is .032 gauge, about 20% thicker.

The accessories are different too. Retail end caps, hangers, and downspout components are made for DIY price points, not durability. They work, but they don't last as long or perform as well.

Sectional vs. Seamless

Here's the fundamental problem: without a gutter machine (which costs $15,000-25,000), you're installing sectional gutters with seams every 10 feet. Those seams are the weakest point in any gutter system.

Metal expands and contracts with temperature changes. Over years of Alabama summers and winters, seams flex and eventually separate. Sealant fails. Leaks develop. PVC gutters are even worse — PVC expands almost 3/4 inch for every 10 feet. The rubber seals in sectional joints break down quickly.

Seamless gutters have seams only at corners and downspouts — about 80% fewer than sectional systems. That means 80% fewer potential leak points.

Safety and Time

Gutter installation means spending hours on a ladder, often at roofline height. For a two-story home, that's 20-25 feet up. Professional crews do this daily with proper equipment and training. A homeowner doing it once or twice risks serious injury.

A professional crew installs 1-3 complete gutter systems per day. They have the equipment, the materials, and the muscle memory. A DIYer using hardware store materials and learning as they go? Days or weeks for a single home — assuming nothing goes wrong.

Our opinion: A complete gutter system costs $1,500-3,500 professionally installed with quality materials and a warranty. That's not cheap, but it's not that expensive either for something that protects your home for 20+ years. The math doesn't usually favor DIY when you factor in time, material quality, and risk.

When DIY Might Make Sense

Small repairs on existing gutters? Sure. Replacing a short section or adding an extension? Probably fine. But a full gutter installation on a house? Get estimates from professionals first. You might be surprised how reasonable the cost is compared to doing it yourself.
