Underground Drains

Why Your Underground Downspout Drain is Clogged and What to Do About It

2020-05-26 4 min read

Why Underground Drains Matter

Underground drainage routes water from your downspouts to a discharge point well away from your foundation — ideally 20-50 feet or more. It's the best solution for foundation protection, yard appearance, and managing water on sloped lots. But when underground drains clog, they're a headache to diagnose and fix.

Common Causes of Clogged Underground Drains

Physical Damage to the Pipe

Underground pipes can be damaged in several ways:

  • Crushed by vehicles or heavy equipment driving over them
  • Cut or broken during other digging projects (fence posts, electrical work, sprinkler systems)
  • Cracked from tree roots growing against them
  • Deteriorated over time from debris flowing through

When pipes crack or break, dirt and debris enter through the openings. The pipe fills with soil, and water backs up toward the house.

Warning from experience: When contractors damage underground drains during other work, they often "fix" it with duct tape or similar quick patches. These repairs fail within months. If someone's been digging near your drain lines and you suddenly have problems, there's a connection.

Perforated Pipe (The Silent Killer)

This is probably the most common cause of underground drain failure we see — and it's usually the original installer's fault.

Some contractors use perforated drain pipe (the kind with slits or holes along its length) for underground gutter drainage. The theory: excess water can escape through the perforations and soak into the soil. The reality: those perforations become root entry points.

Roots seek water. When they detect moisture coming through perforations, they grow toward it. Small root tendrils enter through the holes, then grow larger inside the pipe. Over a few years, the pipe becomes packed with roots. Each rain adds more water, roots grow larger, and eventually the pipe is completely blocked.

Corrugated Flexible Pipe

The other installer shortcut that causes problems: corrugated flex pipe instead of smooth PVC. It costs less upfront and is easier to install — you can snake it around obstacles without fittings.

The problem: those ridges trap debris. Small particles settle into the valleys, building up over time. Roots infiltrate the corrugations more easily than smooth pipe. Within 3-5 years, corrugated pipe commonly clogs — even without root problems.

Our strong opinion: Don't let anyone install corrugated flex pipe for gutter drainage. Spend the extra 20-30% for smooth PVC. You'll avoid expensive excavation and replacement in a few years.

Slow Water Flow and Sediment Buildup

Underground drains work by gravity — water flows downhill through the pipe. If the pipe doesn't have enough slope (about 1 inch per 10 feet), water flows too slowly to flush debris through.

Over years, sediment settles at low spots in the pipe. The buildup grows gradually — roof grit, decomposed leaves, fine debris that made it past your gutters. Eventually the pipe narrows enough to cause backup during heavy rain.

Long runs are especially susceptible. A 50-foot drain needs 5 inches of elevation change to maintain proper flow. If the terrain doesn't provide that naturally, the pipe ends up with flat sections where sediment accumulates.

Diagnosing Clogged Underground Drains

Symptoms of underground drain problems:

  • Water pooling at the base of downspouts during rain
  • Gutters overflowing even when clean
  • Water backing up and exiting where the pipe connects to the downspout
  • Soggy spots in the yard along the pipe route
  • No water coming out at the discharge point during rain

To test: disconnect the downspout from the underground drain and run water from a garden hose directly into the pipe. If water backs up and exits the pipe entrance rather than flowing through, you have a clog.

Repair vs. Replace

When to Try Clearing

If the clog is sediment accumulation (not roots, not physical damage), you can sometimes clear it:

  1. High-pressure water jetting can blast through sediment clogs
  2. Drain snakes can break up soft blockages
  3. Regular flushing with a garden hose can maintain flow if done before severe buildup

Success depends on clog severity and pipe condition. Minor sediment buildup often clears with effort. Major blockages usually require excavation.

When to Replace

Replace the pipe when:

  • Root infiltration has occurred (roots always grow back)
  • The pipe is physically damaged (cracks let in more soil)
  • Perforated pipe was used (switch to solid pipe)
  • Corrugated pipe was used (switch to smooth PVC)
  • Repeated clogs despite clearing (the underlying problem isn't fixable)

The reality: If your underground drain has clogged due to roots or pipe type, repairing instead of replacing usually means dealing with the same problem again within a few years. Excavating once with quality materials is usually cheaper than excavating twice.

Preventing Future Clogs

For North Alabama homes with underground drainage:

  • Use gutter guards: The less debris entering your gutters, the less reaches your underground drains
  • Flush annually: Disconnect downspouts and run a hose through the drain pipe once a year to push out accumulated sediment
  • Avoid planting near drain lines: Tree and shrub roots will find drain pipes
  • Know your pipe locations: Mark them before any digging project
  • Use proper pipe: Smooth PVC, not corrugated. Solid, not perforated.
